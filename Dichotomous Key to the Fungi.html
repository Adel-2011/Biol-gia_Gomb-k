Dichotomous Key to the Fungi

32 fungi and fungus-like organisms — slime molds, water molds, and true fungi

Dichotomous key

1

a. At some stage the organism exists as a moving, wall-less mass of protoplasm (a plasmodium); spores develop later without true hyphae → 2

b. The organism never forms a plasmodium; it grows as thread-like hyphae or as single, wall-bound cells → 5

2

a. Fruiting body one single large, cushion-like mass, bright egg-yolk yellow when fresh ("dog vomit slime mold") → Box I

b. Fruiting bodies not a single large yellow cushion → 3

3

a. Many small, stalked or clustered sporangia; the plasmodium is often white, cream, or yellow before fruiting → Box II

b. Fruiting body not a cluster of small stalked sporangia → 4

4

a. Small pink to coral globules on rotting wood, oozing a pink paste when young, turning gray-brown and powdery with age ("wolf's milk") → Box III

b. A whitish-gray, crust-like layer containing lime (calcium carbonate) granules, usually on grass or low vegetation → Box IV

5

a. Cell walls made mainly of cellulose, not chitin; often produces motile zoospores; aquatic or parasitic on plants → 6

b. Cell walls made of chitin; no motile zoospores produced → 9

6

a. Aquatic; grows as a cottony white mycelium on dead fish, insects, or other decaying matter in water → Box V

b. Not aquatic on dead animals → 7

7

a. Causes damping-off and root rot of seedlings; cottony mycelium develops in wet soil or water around the roots → Box VI

b. Obligate parasite on the leaves of living plants; a downy or felty growth appears on the leaf surface → 8

8

a. Late blight of potato and tomato: dark brown-black lesions on leaves and stems, white downy mold on the leaf underside in humid weather, tubers also rot → Box VII

b. Downy mildew: grayish-violet felty growth on the leaf underside following the veins, matching yellow patches above → Box VIII

9

a. Hyphae coenocytic (few or no cross-walls); fast-growing, woolly gray-white mold typical on bread, fruit, or dung → 10

b. Hyphae divided by regular cross-walls (septa) → 11

10

a. Sporangiophores unbranched, arising singly or in small groups directly from the mycelium; no stolons or rhizoids ("pin mold") → Box IX

b. Mycelium with arching stolons and root-like rhizoids; sporangia turn black at maturity ("black bread mold") → Box X

11

a. Sexual spores (ascospores, usually 8) form inside a sac-like cell, the ascus → 12

b. Sexual spores (basidiospores, usually 4) form externally on a club-shaped cell, the basidium → 22

12

a. Unicellular for most of its life cycle, reproducing by budding; no true mycelium formed → 13

b. Forms a true, thread-like, multicellular mycelium → 14

13

a. Non-pathogenic; used in baking and brewing; ferments sugar to carbon dioxide and alcohol → Box XI

b. Opportunistic human pathogen, causing candidiasis (thrush) → Box XII

14

a. Reproduces mainly by powdery asexual spores (conidia) on simple conidiophores; no large fleshy fruiting body → 15

b. Produces a larger, macroscopic sexual fruiting body or a resting structure → 19

15

a. Conidiophores brush-shaped (a "penicillus"); colonies typically blue-green → Box XIII

b. Conidiophores not brush-shaped → 16

16

a. Conidiophore ends in a swollen vesicle with spore chains radiating outward; colony green, black, or yellow → Box XIV

b. A plant pathogen causing rot or a powdery/felty coating → 17

17

a. Soft, gray, fuzzy mold on ripening fruit such as grapes or strawberries ("gray mold") → Box XV

b. Not a gray fuzzy mold on soft fruit → 18

18

a. Brown rot of stone fruit; tan-brown spore tufts form in concentric rings on the mummified fruit → Box XVI

b. Powdery mildew; a white, powder-like coating covers leaves, shoots, or buds → Box XVII

19

a. Hard, purple-black, banana-shaped resting structure (a sclerotium, or "ergot") replaces individual grain kernels, usually in rye → Box XVIII

b. Fruiting body is not a sclerotium replacing grain kernels → 20

20

a. Fruiting body develops entirely underground (hypogeous); solid and knobby, ball-shaped ("truffle") → Box XIX

b. Fruiting body develops above ground → 21

21

a. Cap irregularly folded and convoluted, brain-like, reddish-brown; contains toxins and needs careful preparation ("false morel") → Box XX

b. Fruiting body a simple cup or saucer shape → Box XXI

22

a. Plant parasite that replaces host tissue with dusty masses of spores; no fleshy fruiting body is ever formed → 23

b. Forms its own visible fruiting body — a mushroom, bracket, or similar structure → 24

23

a. Infects maize; large gray-black galls replace the kernels, later bursting to release powdery black spores ("corn smut") → Box XXII

b. Infects cereals and grasses; orange to rust-brown powdery pustules erupt on stems and leaves ("cereal rust") → Box XXIII

24

a. Fruiting body very hard, woody, hoof-shaped, growing for years on tree trunks ("tinder fungus") → Box XXIV

b. Fruiting body fleshy, not hard and woody → 25

25

a. Begins as a whitish "egg"; the mature cap is covered in a slimy, foul-smelling, dark spore mass on a white stalk ("stinkhorn") → Box XXV

b. Does not develop from an "egg"; no foul-smelling slime → 26

26

a. Grows in dense clusters at the base of trees or stumps; cap honey-colored, often scaly; black shoelace-like rhizomorphs may be found under the bark → Box XXVI

b. Does not grow in dense clusters with black rhizomorphs → 27

27

a. Funnel-shaped, egg-yolk yellow throughout; underside with blunt, forking ridges (false gills), not true gills → Box XXVII

b. Underside of the cap bears true gills or a spongy layer of pores → 28

28

a. Underside a spongy layer of fine pores, not gills; stem thick, often bulbous; cap brown ("porcini") → Box XXVIII

b. Underside of the cap bears true, blade-like gills → 29

29

a. Base of the stem enclosed in a persistent, cup-like sheath (a volva) → 30

b. No volva at the base of the stem → 31

30

a. Cap greenish-yellow, smooth; gills and spore print white; extremely poisonous, causing fatal liver damage ("death cap") → Box XXIX

b. Cap bright red, covered with small white wart-like patches; contains a hallucinogenic toxin ("fly agaric") → Box XXX

31

a. Cap very large (30–40 cm), covered with coarse brown scales; stem slender with a movable double ring ("parasol mushroom") → Box XXXI

b. Cap and stem smaller, a typical mushroom shape; gills change from pink to dark brown as spores mature; a simple ring on the stem → Box XXXII
